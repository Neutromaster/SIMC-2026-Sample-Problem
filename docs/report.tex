\begin{abstract}
We treat the question \emph{``which students colluded during a test?''} as a
geometry problem on the design matrix $\X\in\{-1,+1\}^{M\times N}$. Tasks~1
and~2 visualise $\X$ and the Gram matrix $\Sm = \X\X^{\!\top}$, immediately
exposing one pair of students (indices $5$ and $10$) whose answer vectors
are \emph{identical}. Task~3 applies $K$-means to recover the underlying
group structure, and we introduce a \textbf{singleton-filtered silhouette
criterion} that resolves the well-known tendency of the silhouette score
to reward over-fragmentation. Task~4 makes the qualitative case for
collusion. Task~5 derives the closed-form mean
$\langle S\rangle = N(2p-1)^2$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_N = \sqrt{N\bigl[1 - (2p-1)^4\bigr]}$ of the similarity score under
a null hypothesis of independent test-takers. Task~6 validates that closed
form by Monte Carlo across a 25-cell $(N,p)$ grid, recovers the exact
shifted-binomial distribution of $S$ under the null, and turns the $(5,10)$
pair's similarity into a calibrated tail probability of
$\,4.2\times 10^{-8}$ -- the shortlist becomes a verdict.
Task~7 promotes the closed-form moments into a full Gaussian null
distribution via the Lindeberg--Lévy CLT, yielding analytical
continuity-corrected $p$-values.
Task~8 scales the pipeline to the larger
$M\!=\!10{,}000$, $N\!=\!50$ dataset where the homogeneous-$p$ assumption
fails: we estimate per-question difficulties $p_k$, generalise the null
moments via the Lindeberg--Feller CLT, and compute the \emph{exact}
shifted Poisson-binomial PMF of $S$ in closed form. A pair scan at
threshold $S\!\geq\!40$ flags ten pairs, all at the theoretical maximum
$S\!=\!50$; they are the $\binom{5}{2}\!=\!10$ pairwise comparisons inside a
\textbf{five-student clique with byte-identical answer vectors}
(one-based IDs $86, 1352, 1907, 3543, 5363$) whose exact null tail
probability is $6.4\times 10^{-15}$ per pair -- a $6.8\sigma$ event after
the natural multiple-comparisons baseline of $\sim\!5\times 10^7$ candidate
pairs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Task 1 -- The Design Matrix}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Definition and encoding}
Let there be $M$ students and $N$ questions. The \emph{design matrix} is
\begin{equation}
  \X \;\equiv\; X_{ij} \;\in\; \{-1,+1\}^{M\times N},
  \qquad
  X_{ij} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +1, & \text{student $i$ answered question $j$ correctly,}\\
    -1, & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
The symmetric $\pm 1$ encoding (rather than $\{0,1\}$) is deliberate: it
makes Eq.~\eqref{eq:design} a sign-vector representation, so that the
inner product of two rows directly measures \emph{net agreement} (see
Task~2).

\subsection{Visualisation}
Figure~\ref{fig:task1} shows the small subset
\texttt{sample\_small}$\in\{-1,+1\}^{50\times 25}$ rendered as a binary
heatmap, with marginal bar plots of the per-student total
$\sum_j X_{ij}$ and per-question total $\sum_i X_{ij}$.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{../data/output/task1/task1_design_matrix.png}
  \caption{Design matrix for \texttt{sample\_small} ($M=50$, $N=25$).
  Black cells encode wrong answers, white cells encode correct answers.
  Right margin: student net scores. Bottom margin: question net scores.
  Overall accuracy is \SI{55.8}{\percent}.}
  \label{fig:task1}
\end{figure}

\begin{insight}[What the marginals already tell us]
The per-question bars expose a markedly uneven test: questions
$0$, $7$ and $23$ behave like ``free'' questions (net positive $> +35$),
while questions $19$--$22$ are net negative. \emph{Question difficulty is
not uniform.} Task~8 will exploit exactly this fact by estimating
per-question $p_j$ from column means before applying the null model.
\end{insight}

\section{Task 2 -- The Similarity Matrix}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Construction}
Define the \emph{pairwise similarity matrix} $\Sm \in \mathbb{Z}^{M\times M}$
by
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij} \;\equiv\; (\X\X^{\!\top})_{ij}
         \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N} X_{ik}\,X_{jk}.
  \label{eq:sim}
\end{equation}
Because each factor is $\pm 1$, the contribution of question $k$ to
$S_{ij}$ is
\begin{equation}
  X_{ik}X_{jk} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +1, & \text{students $i$ and $j$ \textbf{agreed} on question $k$,}\\
    -1, & \text{students $i$ and $j$ \textbf{disagreed} on question $k$.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:sim-term}
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij} \;=\; \#\text{agreements} \;-\; \#\text{disagreements}
         \;\in\; \{-N,-N+2,\ldots,N\},
\end{equation}
with the diagonal pinned at $S_{ii} = N$ by construction (a student
always agrees with themselves on every question).

\subsection{Geometric interpretation}
Each row $\X_{i,\cdot}$ has constant Euclidean norm $\lVert\X_{i,\cdot}\rVert
= \sqrt{N}$, so $\Sm$ is, up to a constant, a \emph{cosine similarity}
matrix:
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta_{ij}
  \;=\; \frac{\langle\X_{i,\cdot},\X_{j,\cdot}\rangle}
             {\lVert\X_{i,\cdot}\rVert\,\lVert\X_{j,\cdot}\rVert}
  \;=\; \frac{S_{ij}}{N}.
  \label{eq:cos}
\end{equation}
This is the key bridge to clustering algorithms (Task~3): \emph{any}
distance-based clustering on the rows of $\X$ implicitly partitions
students by cosine similarity, i.e.\ by $\Sm$.

\subsection{Visualisation and outliers}
Figure~\ref{fig:task2} plots $\Sm$ as a diverging heatmap together with
the histogram of all $\binom{M}{2} = 1225$ unique off-diagonal entries.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{../data/output/task2/task2_similarity_matrix.png}
  \caption{Left: similarity matrix $\Sm = \X\X^{\!\top}$ for
  \texttt{sample\_small}. The diagonal $S_{ii}=N=25$ is dark red.
  Right: histogram of off-diagonal $S_{ij}$ with $i<j$.}
  \label{fig:task2}
\end{figure}

\begin{keyresult}[A single pair sits at the theoretical maximum]
The maximum off-diagonal similarity is $S_{5,10} = 25 = N$, meaning
students $5$ and $10$ \textbf{agreed on all 25 questions}. The bulk of
the histogram has mean $\bar S_{\text{off}} \approx 2.89$ with a roughly
Gaussian spread, so the $(5,10)$ entry is a striking outlier in the
right tail.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Task 3 -- Clustering with $K$-means and Silhouette Analysis}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Algorithm}
$K$-means partitions $\{\X_{i,\cdot}\}_{i=1}^{M}$ into $K$ disjoint
groups by alternately
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item assigning each student to the cluster whose centroid is
        closest, and
  \item recomputing centroids as the mean of their assigned members,
\end{enumerate}
until centroids stabilise. To mitigate sensitivity to the random
initial centroids we use scikit-learn's \texttt{n\_init=20}.

\subsection{Visualising cluster quality}
For each $K$ we render $\Sm$ with its rows and columns permuted by
cluster label. A good $K$ produces solid red squares on the diagonal
(in-group agreement) against a paler background (out-group
disagreement). Figure~\ref{fig:task3-sweep} shows three representative
values.

\subsection{Silhouette score}
For a single point $i$ assigned to cluster $C(i)$, let $a(i)$ be its
mean (cosine) distance to other in-cluster points and
$b(i)=\min_{C\neq C(i)}\bar d(i,C)$ its mean distance to the nearest
\emph{foreign} cluster. The silhouette is
\begin{equation}
  s(i) \;=\; \frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),b(i)\}}\;\in\;[-1,1],
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Silh}(K) \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} s(i).
  \label{eq:silh}
\end{equation}
Because our rows have constant norm, the natural distance is
$d_{\cos}(i,j) = 1 - S_{ij}/N$ -- the silhouette is computed in the same
geometry as $\Sm$ itself.

\subsection{The silhouette trap and our fix}
\label{sec:silh-trap}
A naive ``pick the $K$ that maximises $\mathrm{Silh}(K)$'' rule selects
$K^\star_{\text{raw}} = 30$ for our data, with
$\mathrm{Silh}(30) = 0.186$. But $K=30$ has \textbf{17 of 30 clusters
containing a single student}, the very pathology the problem statement
flags as ``defeating the purpose of clustering''. The mechanism is
mechanical:

\begin{insight}[Why silhouette over-rewards singletons]
A singleton cluster has $a(i)$ \emph{undefined} and the convention sets
$s(i)=0$, but every \emph{non-singleton} member of any other cluster
gains a more distant ``nearest foreign cluster'' as singletons are
peeled off, inflating their $b(i)$. The aggregate
$\mathrm{Silh}(K)$ therefore tends to drift upward with $K$ even after
the partition has lost meaning.
\end{insight}

We therefore use a \textbf{singleton-filtered silhouette criterion}:
\begin{equation}
  K^\star \;=\; \arg\max_{K\;:\;\min_c |C_c|\,\geq\,2}\;\mathrm{Silh}(K).
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:silh} summarises the sweep.

\section{Task 4 -- A Plausibility Argument for Collusion}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Can we already name names? Honestly, no.}
Tasks~1--3 give us a heatmap, a Gram matrix, and a partition into
$K^\star = 10$ clusters. None of these tools is a \emph{detector} of
cheating. The design matrix tells us only \emph{what} each student
answered; it does not tell us \emph{how} they came to that answer.
Two students might score identically because:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item one copied from the other;
  \item they prepared together from the same notes and learned the same
        gaps;
  \item they happen to be of similar ability on similar topics; or
  \item pure luck on a short test.
\end{enumerate}
Without a probability model we cannot rank these explanations against
each other. So any sentence of the form \emph{``students $i$ and $j$
colluded''} is, at this stage, an overreach. The honest conclusion is
weaker but still useful: \emph{some pairs look unlike what we would
naively expect, and they deserve a closer look}.

\subsection{Shift the goal: suspects, not convicts}
A useful change of framing is to stop trying to convict anyone and
start trying to \emph{shortlist suspects}. The two tasks have very
different evidentiary requirements:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Conviction} demands a low-error decision rule, e.g.\
        a calibrated $p$-value below some threshold. The penalty for
        wrongly accusing an honest student is severe, so we cannot
        afford false positives.
  \item \textbf{Suspect-flagging} only demands that the shortlist
        contains the truly suspicious pairs. False positives on the
        shortlist are cheap: a human grader can manually inspect a
        dozen flagged pairs in minutes.
\end{itemize}
For a class of $M = 50$ students there are $\binom{50}{2} = 1225$
distinct pairs -- far too many to review by hand. But if we can use
Tasks~1--3 to narrow $1225$ pairs down to a shortlist of, say, the
top $5$ or $10$, then a teacher or invigilator can examine those
specific cases directly. \textbf{We are doing triage, not jurisprudence.}
Task~5 onwards will earn us a real test of significance; for now,
qualitative anomalies are enough to populate the shortlist.

\subsection{Where would collusion ``leak'' into our analysis?}
Even without a null model, three independent fingerprints in our
existing analyses point toward the same suspect pair, and all three
are the \emph{kind} of pattern we would expect collusion to leave
behind.

\paragraph{Fingerprint 1 -- impossibly perfect agreement on a non-trivial
test.} The pair $(i,j) = (5,10)$ has $S_{5,10} = +25$. Because
$S_{ij} = N - 2\cdot(\#\text{disagreements})$, this is only possible
when the two students disagree on \emph{zero} questions. On a uniform
test where every answer is a coin flip, the chance of zero
disagreements over $N$ questions is $\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^N$ -- already
$1/3.4\times 10^7$ for $N=25$. Realistic tests have some easy
questions where two honest students naturally agree, which raises the
probability, but the qualitative point survives: \emph{perfect
agreement is the regime where coincidence runs out of room}.

\paragraph{Fingerprint 2 -- agreement on the \emph{hard} questions as
well as the easy ones.} Figure~\ref{fig:task1} shows that question
difficulty in this test is markedly non-uniform: questions $0$, $7$,
$23$ are nearly-free (large positive column sums) while questions
$19$--$22$ are net-negative (most students get them wrong). Two honest
high-achievers should agree on the easy questions but their answer
patterns on the hard questions should diverge, because hard questions
are the ones where students' independent reasoning paths produce
different mistakes. Collusion erases that divergence: copied wrong
answers look identical. The fact that students $5$ and $10$ agree on
\emph{every} question, easy and hard alike, is the
``honest-students-shouldn't-look-like-this'' signature.

\paragraph{Fingerprint 3 -- crystalline cluster co-membership.} Honest
similarity is \emph{fuzzy}: as we sweep $K$ in $K$-means, an honest
pair's cluster co-membership flickers on and off depending on how the
boundaries are drawn. A colluding pair, by contrast, has effectively
identical feature vectors, so they sit on top of each other in feature
space and remain co-clustered \emph{across every choice of $K$}. In
our sweep $K \in \{2,3,5,8,10,12,15,20,25,30,40\}$, students $5$ and
$10$ never separated. That ``always together'' behaviour is exactly
the geometric signature of two students who share a feature vector.

\subsection{The data behind the shortlist}
Combining the empirical similarity distribution from Task~2 with the
clustering output from Task~3 gives Table~\ref{tab:suspects}, our
working suspect shortlist for \texttt{sample\_small}.

In words: out of $1225$ candidate pairs of students, exactly one --
the $(5,10)$ pair -- sits at the theoretical maximum $S = N = 25$,
roughly $3.5$ empirical standard deviations above the off-diagonal
mean, and persists in the same cluster under every choice of $K$ we
tried. \textbf{That one pair is our suspect.} Whether it is also a
conviction-grade case requires the statistical machinery built in
Tasks~5--7.

\begin{insight}[Hint from the problem statement]
The problem explicitly warns ``you might not actually get too far''
without a statistical null model. Our reading is sharper: we can
\emph{shortlist} (we just did), but we cannot \emph{prove}. The proof
step -- attaching a probability to the observed $S = 25$ -- is
exactly what Task~5 sets up by deriving the null-model mean and
standard deviation, which Tasks~6 and~7 then turn into a calibrated
$p$-value.
\end{insight}

% ==================================================================
\section{Task 5 -- Mean and Variance of the Similarity Score
                    Under the Null Hypothesis}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{What are we actually computing, and why?}
Imagine two students who took the test \emph{honestly} -- no copying,
no shared answers. Their similarity score $S$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:sim}
will be some number. If we could repeat this scenario for many pairs
of honest students, we would get a whole distribution of $S$ values.
Task~5 asks for two summary numbers of that distribution:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item the \textbf{mean} $\langle S\rangle$ -- the average value of
        $S$ across all honest pairs;
  \item the \textbf{standard deviation} $\sigma_N$ -- how much $S$
        typically wobbles around that mean.
\end{itemize}
These two numbers give us a ``ruler'': a real pair whose $S$ is far
above $\langle S\rangle + \text{(a few)}\,\sigma_N$ is suspicious,
because honest students almost never score that high.

\subsection{The probability model (one student, one question)}
Assume each student answers each question independently, getting it
right with probability $p$ and wrong with probability $1-p$. In
$\pm 1$ language,
\begin{equation}
  X_{ik} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +1 & \text{with probability }p \quad\text{(right)},\\
    -1 & \text{with probability }1-p \quad\text{(wrong)}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
``Independently'' means knowing one answer tells us nothing about
any other answer. This is the assumption that lets us add things up
later.

\subsection{Two students, one question: agree or disagree?}
Pick two distinct students $i$ and $j$ and one question $k$. Define
the per-question \emph{agreement indicator}
\begin{equation}
  Y_k \;\equiv\; X_{ik}\,X_{jk}.
  \label{eq:Yk}
\end{equation}
Why this product? Because of the $\pm 1$ encoding, $Y_k$ is a clean
yes/no signal:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item $Y_k = (+1)(+1) = +1$ if both got it right -- \emph{agree};
  \item $Y_k = (-1)(-1) = +1$ if both got it wrong -- \emph{also agree};
  \item $Y_k = (+1)(-1) = -1$ if one was right, the other wrong -- \emph{disagree};
  \item $Y_k = (-1)(+1) = -1$ -- \emph{also disagree}.
\end{itemize}
Listing the four possible joint outcomes with their probabilities
makes it concrete (Table~\ref{tab:fourcases}).

\begin{table}[htb]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{c c c c}
    \toprule
    student $i$ & student $j$ & joint probability & $Y_k$ \\
    \midrule
    right & right & $p\cdot p = p^2$            & $+1$ (agree) \\
    right & wrong & $p\cdot(1-p)$               & $-1$ (disagree) \\
    wrong & right & $(1-p)\cdot p$              & $-1$ (disagree) \\
    wrong & wrong & $(1-p)\cdot(1-p) = (1-p)^2$ & $+1$ (agree) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{All four ways two students can answer one question, with
  the probability of each combination and the resulting $Y_k$.}
  \label{tab:fourcases}
\end{table}

Adding the rows of Table~\ref{tab:fourcases} that give the same
$Y_k$,
\begin{align}
  \Pr[\,Y_k = +1\,] &\;=\; p^2 + (1-p)^2 \qquad\text{(both rows that agree),} \label{eq:pY+1}\\
  \Pr[\,Y_k = -1\,] &\;=\; 2\,p(1-p)     \qquad\text{(both rows that disagree).} \label{eq:pY-1}
\end{align}
\textbf{Quick check.} At $p = \tfrac12$:
$\Pr[Y_k=+1] = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$ and
$\Pr[Y_k=-1] = 2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$. Random students
agree exactly half the time -- as expected.

\subsection{The average of a single $Y_k$}
The average of $Y_k$ is each possible value weighted by its
probability:
\begin{equation}
  \Exp[Y_k] \;=\; (+1)\cdot\Pr[Y_k=+1] \;+\; (-1)\cdot\Pr[Y_k=-1].
\end{equation}
Plug in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:pY+1}--\eqref{eq:pY-1} and expand step by step:
\begin{align}
  \Exp[Y_k]
   &\;=\; \bigl[p^2 + (1-p)^2\bigr] \;-\; 2p(1-p) \notag\\
   &\;=\; p^2 \;-\; 2p(1-p) \;+\; (1-p)^2.
   \label{eq:EYexpand}
\end{align}
The right-hand side is the perfect square
$a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a-b)^2$ with $a = p$ and $b = 1-p$, so
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Exp[Y_k] \;=\; \bigl[p - (1-p)\bigr]^2 \;=\; (2p-1)^2.\;}
  \label{eq:EY}
\end{equation}
Notice $(2p-1)$ is the gap between the chance of being right and the
chance of being wrong. At $p = \tfrac12$ this gap is zero, so
$\Exp[Y_k] = 0$ -- agreements and disagreements cancel on average.
At $p = 1$ this gap is $1$, so $\Exp[Y_k] = 1$ -- everyone always
agrees.

\subsection{The variance of a single $Y_k$ -- a one-line trick}
Variance asks: how far does $Y_k$ typically sit from its mean? The
defining formula is
\begin{equation}
  \Var(Y_k) \;=\; \Exp[Y_k^2] \;-\; \bigl(\Exp[Y_k]\bigr)^2.
  \label{eq:vardef}
\end{equation}
Here is the trick. Because $Y_k$ is always either $+1$ or $-1$, its
\emph{square} is always $1$:
\begin{equation}
  Y_k^2 \;\equiv\; 1
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \Exp[Y_k^2] \;=\; 1.
  \label{eq:EYsq}
\end{equation}
Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:EY} and~\eqref{eq:EYsq} into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:vardef}:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Var(Y_k) \;=\; 1 \;-\; (2p-1)^4.\;}
  \label{eq:VarY}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Putting all $N$ questions together}
The total similarity is just the sum of one $Y_k$ per question:
\begin{equation}
  S \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N} Y_k \;=\; Y_1 + Y_2 + \cdots + Y_N.
  \label{eq:Ssum}
\end{equation}
Two simple probability rules now finish the job.

\paragraph{Rule 1 -- the mean of a sum is the sum of the means.}
This is \emph{always} true, no independence needed:
\begin{equation}
  \langle S\rangle \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N}\Exp[Y_k]
                   \;=\; N\cdot(2p-1)^2.
  \label{eq:ES}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Rule 2 -- for \emph{independent} terms, the variance of a
sum is the sum of the variances.} The independence in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:null} carries over to the $Y_k$'s, so
\begin{equation}
  \Var(S) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N}\Var(Y_k)
          \;=\; N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr].
  \label{eq:VarS}
\end{equation}
The standard deviation is the positive square root,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_N \;=\; \sqrt{\Var(S)} \;=\; \sqrt{N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr]}.
  \label{eq:sigmaN}
\end{equation}

\begin{keyresult}[Final answer to Task~5]
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \langle S\rangle \;=\; N\,(2p-1)^2,
  \qquad
  \sigma_N \;=\; \sqrt{N\bigl[\,1 - (2p-1)^4\,\bigr]}.
  \;}
  \label{eq:final}
\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{A worked numerical example}
Suppose $p = 0.6$ (a moderately easy test) and $N = 25$ questions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item Mean: $\langle S\rangle = 25\cdot(2\cdot 0.6 - 1)^2
        = 25\cdot(0.2)^2 = 25\cdot 0.04 = 1.0$.
  \item Variance:
        $\Var(S) = 25\cdot[1 - (0.2)^4] = 25\cdot[1 - 0.0016] = 24.96$.
  \item Standard deviation:
        $\sigma_N = \sqrt{24.96} \approx 4.996 \approx 5.0$.
\end{itemize}
So between two honest students on a $25$-question test where the
average accuracy is $60\%$, expect their similarity score to sit
around $1$ with a typical wobble of $\pm 5$. A pair scoring $S = 20$
would already be $(20 - 1)/5 \approx 3.8$ standard deviations above
the honest-pair average -- worth investigating.

\subsection{Bringing the null model back to the data}
For \texttt{sample\_small} we observed an overall accuracy
$\hat p \approx 0.558$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:task1}). Plugging into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:final} with $N=25$ gives the \emph{null} expectations
\begin{equation}
  \langle S\rangle_{\text{null}}
  \;\approx\; 25(2(0.558)-1)^2 \;\approx\; 0.34,
  \qquad
  \sigma_N \;\approx\; \sqrt{25\bigl[1-(0.116)^4\bigr]}\;\approx\;5.0.
\end{equation}
The observed pair $(5,10)$ scored $S=25$, i.e.\
$(25-0.34)/5.0 \approx 4.9\,\sigma_N$ above the null mean. In words:
\textbf{under the null hypothesis of independent test-takers, the
agreement we see between students $5$ and $10$ would be a $\sim 5\sigma$
event}. Tasks~6 (Monte-Carlo confirmation) and~7 (central-limit-theorem
distribution of $S$) will sharpen this informal estimate into a real
$p$-value, and Task~8 will replay the same machinery on the much larger
\texttt{sample\_larger} ($M=10{,}000$, $N=50$) where per-question
difficulties $p_j$ must be estimated column-wise.

\section{Task 6 -- Monte Carlo Validation of the Null Moments}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Why simulate something we already proved?}
Task~5 produced two clean closed-form expressions, Eq.~\eqref{eq:final},
for the mean and standard deviation of $S$ under the null hypothesis of
independent test-takers. If the derivation is right and the assumptions
are right, those formulas are \emph{exactly} the moments we should
recover from a simulation. Running a Monte Carlo therefore serves three
distinct purposes:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item It is a \textbf{numerical sanity check} on the algebra of
        Task~5. If the simulated mean and standard deviation disagree
        with Eq.~\eqref{eq:final} at any $(N, p)$, we have a bug
        somewhere upstream.
  \item It produces an empirical distribution of $S$ that we can
        \textbf{compare against the central-limit Gaussian} Task~7 will
        rely on, so we can see whether the CLT approximation is
        accurate at the values of $N$ that matter for the problem.
  \item It earns us a concrete \textbf{tail probability} for the
        observed $(5,10)$ pair, completing the chain
        ``shortlist $\to$ moments $\to$ $p$-value'' begun in Task~4.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Monte Carlo recipe}
We never simulate students in pairs that share a row. The recipe is
the cleanest version of i.i.d.\ sampling:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item Choose $(N, p)$ and a desired number $M$ of pair samples.
  \item Draw a $\{-1,+1\}$ matrix $X \in \{-1,+1\}^{2M \times N}$
        where each entry is independently $+1$ with probability $p$.
  \item Pair the first $M$ rows with the last $M$ rows.
  \item For each pair $i$, compute
        $S^{(i)} = \langle X_{i,\cdot},\, X_{M+i,\cdot}\rangle$ as a
        single vectorised dot product over all $M$ pairs.
  \item Return $\{S^{(1)}, \ldots, S^{(M)}\}$ -- $M$ i.i.d.\ samples
        from the null distribution.
\end{enumerate}
Using i.i.d.\ pairs (rather than the $\binom{2M}{2}$ pairwise
similarities of a single batch of students) keeps the standard error on
the sample mean exactly $\sigma_N / \sqrt{M}$ -- no correlation
correction needed.

\subsection{Headline check: $N = 100$, $p = 1/2$, $M = 10^{6}$}
At $p = 1/2$ the closed form simplifies dramatically:
$\langle S\rangle = 0$ and $\sigma_N = \sqrt{N} = 10$ for $N = 100$.

\subsection{Bonus: the distribution of $S$ is exactly known}
\label{sec:task6-exact}
The simulation incidentally reveals something Task~5 stopped short of
saying. Let $T \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}(S + N) = \#\{k : Y_k = +1\}$ be the
number of agreements over the $N$ questions. Each $Y_k$ is an
independent $\{-1,+1\}$ Bernoulli with $\Pr[Y_k = +1] = q$, where
\begin{equation}
  q \;\equiv\; p^{2} + (1-p)^{2} \;=\; 1 - 2p(1-p).
  \label{eq:q-defn}
\end{equation}
The $Y_k$'s are i.i.d., so $T \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N, q)$ and
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;S \;=\; 2T - N, \qquad T \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N, q).\;}
  \label{eq:exact-S}
\end{equation}
The full PMF of $S$ under the null is therefore known in \emph{closed
form}, not merely its first two moments. Task~7 will replace
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-S} with its CLT Gaussian approximation -- which is
what one wants for an analytical $p$-value at arbitrary $N$ -- but
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-S} is the ground truth and we can use it now to
audit Task~7's accuracy before we commit to it.

Figure~\ref{fig:task6-distribution} overlays three curves at
$(N, p) = (100, 0.5)$: the empirical histogram from the
$10^{6}$-pair simulation, the exact PMF from Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-S},
and the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\langle S\rangle, \sigma_N^{2})$. The
three agree to graphical precision -- so Task~7's CLT is well-justified
at this $N$.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{../data/output/task6/task6_distribution_N100_p05.png}
  \caption{Distribution of $S$ under the null at $(N, p) = (100, 0.5)$.
  Blue bars: $10^{6}$ Monte Carlo samples. Red markers: exact
  shifted-binomial PMF $S = 2T-N$ with
  $T \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N,q)$, $q = p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}$. Black curve:

  \label{fig:task6-distribution}
\end{figure}

\begin{insight}[Why all three curves agree here]
At $p = 1/2$ each $Y_k$ is symmetric about zero and bounded, so the
Lindeberg condition of the CLT is satisfied trivially. The
$\mathrm{Binomial}(N, q)$ with $q = 1/2$ is, by de Moivre--Laplace, the
canonical Gaussian-approachable distribution. At $N = 100$ the
correction terms of the Edgeworth expansion (skewness, excess
kurtosis) are already below graphical precision, which is why the
three curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:task6-distribution} are visually
indistinguishable. For very asymmetric $p$ (say $p = 0.05$) the
agreement would degrade for small $N$, and Task~7's Gaussian would
under-cover the tails.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Closing the loop: the $(5, 10)$ pair under the null}
We finally have every ingredient needed to put a number on the
question that opened this report: \emph{how unlikely is
$S_{5,10}=25$ under the null hypothesis of independent test-takers?}
Substituting the empirical accuracy $\hat p = 0.558$ and $N = 25$ into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:final},
\begin{equation}
  \langle S\rangle_{\text{null}} \;\approx\; 0.336,
  \qquad
  \sigma_N \;\approx\; 5.000,
  \qquad
  z \;\equiv\;
     \frac{S_{5,10}-\langle S\rangle_{\text{null}}}{\sigma_N}
     \;\approx\; 4.93.
  \label{eq:510z}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-S} the \emph{exact} one-sided tail probability
under the null, with $q = 0.558^{2} + 0.442^{2} \approx 0.507$, is
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\bigl[\,S \geq 25 \bigm|\, H_{0}\,\bigr]
  \;=\; \Pr\bigl[\,T \geq 25 \bigm|\, T \sim \mathrm{Bin}(25,\,q)\,\bigr]
  \;\approx\; 4.16\times 10^{-8}.
  \label{eq:510-exact-p}
\end{equation}
The CLT Gaussian approximation, which Task~7 will use for analytical
$p$-values at arbitrary $N$, gives a (continuity-corrected)
$\Pr[S \geq 25] \approx 6.7 \times 10^{-7}$ at this $N$.  The CLT
slightly under-covers the right tail because the exact distribution has
positive skewness when $p \neq 1/2$; for ranking pairs the difference is
inconsequential, and both values sit far below any reasonable
significance threshold.

\begin{keyresult}[The shortlist becomes a verdict]
\textbf{Under the null hypothesis of independent test-takers,
$S_{5,10}=25$ is a $4.93\,\sigma_N$ event with an exact one-sided
probability of roughly $1$ in $24{,}000{,}000$.} Coupled with the
qualitative fingerprints of Task~4, this is conviction-grade evidence
that students $5$ and $10$ did not arrive at identical answer vectors
independently. The Task~5 closed form has been validated numerically
across the $(N,p)$ plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:task6-grid}); the CLT
approximation needed for Task~7 has been visually validated against
both the exact PMF and the empirical histogram
(Fig.~\ref{fig:task6-distribution}); and the canonical suspect pair
finally has a calibrated $p$-value.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Task 7 -- The Null Distribution of $S$ via the
                    Central Limit Theorem}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{From two moments to a full distribution}
Task~5 handed us the mean $\langle S\rangle$ and standard deviation
$\sigma_N$ of the similarity score under the null hypothesis of
independent test-takers. Those two numbers already let us make
informal $z$-score statements (Sec.~5.8), but they are not enough to
attach a calibrated probability to an observed value of $S$. For that
we need the full \emph{shape} of the null distribution, i.e.\ the
function
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[\,S = s\,]
  \qquad\text{for every allowed }s\in\{-N,-N+2,\ldots,N\}.
  \label{eq:goalT7}
\end{equation}
Task~7 derives Eq.~\eqref{eq:goalT7} in closed form by recognising
that $S$ is a sum of $N$ independent, bounded contributions and
invoking the central limit theorem (CLT).

\subsection{Why $S$ is a textbook CLT candidate}
Recall from Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ssum} that the similarity score is the sum
\begin{equation}
  S \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N} Y_k,
  \qquad
  Y_k \;=\; X_{ik}\,X_{jk}\;\in\;\{-1,+1\},
  \label{eq:Srecall}
\end{equation}
of $N$ per-question agreement indicators. The CLT says: a sum of
many independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
with finite variance is approximately Gaussian, regardless of the
distribution of the individual terms. Three conditions matter, and
each holds in our setup:

\begin{insight}[Verifying the Lindeberg--Lévy CLT conditions]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=(\roman*),nosep]
  \item \textbf{Independence.} The null model
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:null}) declares the $X_{ik}$ independent across
        $k$. Independence is preserved under products of
        non-overlapping factors, so
        $Y_k = X_{ik}X_{jk}$ are independent across $k$ as well.
  \item \textbf{Identically distributed.} In the homogeneous-test
        setting of Task~5, every question shares the same difficulty
        $p$, so every $Y_k$ shares the same two-point distribution
        $\bigl(\Pr[Y_k=+1],\Pr[Y_k=-1]\bigr) = \bigl(p^2+(1-p)^2,\,2p(1-p)\bigr)$.
  \item \textbf{Finite variance.} From Eq.~\eqref{eq:VarY},
        $\Var(Y_k) = 1 - (2p-1)^4 \leq 1 < \infty$, since
        $(2p-1)^4 \in [0,1]$ for $p\in[0,1]$.
\end{enumerate}
All three conditions are satisfied. The classical Lindeberg--Lévy
CLT therefore applies.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Statement of the limit theorem}
Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_N$ be i.i.d.\ with mean
$\mu_Y \equiv \Exp[Y_k] = (2p-1)^2$ and variance
$\sigma_Y^2 \equiv \Var(Y_k) = 1-(2p-1)^4$. The Lindeberg--Lévy CLT
states
\begin{equation}
  Z_N
  \;\equiv\;
  \frac{S - N\mu_Y}{\sigma_Y\sqrt{N}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\;
  \mathcal{N}(0,1)
  \qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,
  \label{eq:clt}
\end{equation}
where $\xrightarrow{d}$ denotes convergence in distribution.
Equivalently, in the original scale,
\begin{equation}
  S \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\;
  \mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(N(2p-1)^2,\;\;N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr]\Bigr).
  \label{eq:Sasymp}
\end{equation}
For finite $N$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sasymp} is an \emph{approximation} whose
accuracy improves with $N$. For our parameters ($N=25$ in
\texttt{sample\_small}, $N=50$ in \texttt{sample\_larger}) the
Berry--Esseen bound predicts a Kolmogorov distance to the limiting
Gaussian of $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N}) \lesssim 0.2$ in the worst case
and substantially better in the bulk -- adequate for tail tests at
the level we care about, and verified empirically against Task~6
Monte Carlo.

\subsection{From the Gaussian density to a probability mass}
\label{sec:lattice}
The CLT in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sasymp} gives a \emph{continuous} density
\begin{equation}
  \varphi(s)
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\sigma_N^2}}\;
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(s - \langle S\rangle)^2}{2\sigma_N^2}\right],
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
but $S$ is discrete. Two structural facts about the lattice on which
$S$ lives turn $\varphi(s)$ into the probability mass we actually
want:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Parity.} Each $Y_k \in \{-1,+1\}$ changes $S$ by $\pm 1$
        relative to $-N$ (its minimum), so $S + N$ is always even and
        therefore $S$ has the same parity as $N$.
  \item \textbf{Spacing.} Flipping a single $Y_k$ from $+1$ to $-1$
        changes $S$ by exactly $2$. Consecutive allowed values of $S$
        are separated by $\Delta s = 2$, never by $1$.
\end{itemize}
The probability of landing on a specific allowed value $s$ is the
density times the lattice spacing:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[\,S = s\,] \;\approx\; \varphi(s)\cdot\Delta s
                       \;=\; 2\,\varphi(s).
  \label{eq:lattice}
\end{equation}
For tail probabilities (which is what we will use in the hypothesis
test), the discrete-to-continuous correction is handled by a
half-spacing shift,
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[\,S \geq s\,]
  \;\approx\;
  1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{(s-1) - \langle S\rangle}{\sigma_N}\right),
  \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal CDF and $s-1$ implements
the standard continuity correction at the lattice spacing of $2$
(half-step $= 1$).

\subsection{Closed-form null distribution}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:phi}--\eqref{eq:lattice} with the Task~5
moments gives the final pointwise distribution.

\begin{keyresult}[Null distribution of $S$ (homogeneous $p$)]
For two independent students answering $N$ questions, each with
common difficulty $p$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \Pr[\,S=s\,]
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi\,N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr]}}\;
  \exp\!\left[
    -\,\frac{\bigl(s - N(2p-1)^2\bigr)^2}
            {2\,N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr]}
  \right],
  \;}
  \label{eq:T7-mass}
\end{equation}
on the lattice $s\in\{-N,-N+2,\ldots,N-2,N\}$. The corresponding
upper-tail probability is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \Pr[\,S \geq s_{\text{obs}}\,]
  \;\approx\;
  1 - \Phi\!\left(
    \frac{s_{\text{obs}} - 1 - N(2p-1)^2}
         {\sqrt{N\bigl[1-(2p-1)^4\bigr]}}
  \right).
  \;}
  \label{eq:T7-tail}
\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Sanity checks at the extremes}
The closed form in Eq.~\eqref{eq:T7-mass} reduces correctly in every
limit Task~5 already covered, plus one new shape-level check.

\begin{table}[htb]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{c c c l}
    \toprule
    regime & $\langle S\rangle$ & $\sigma_N^2$ &
        shape of $\Pr[S=s]$ \\
    \midrule
    $p = \tfrac12$ & $0$ & $N$ &
        symmetric Gaussian on $\pm 1$ random walk \\
    $p\to 0$ or $p\to 1$ & $N$ & $0$ &
        Dirac mass at $s=N$ (Gaussian collapses) \\
    $N\to\infty$ at fixed $p$ & $\sim N(2p-1)^2$ & $\sim N$ &
        relative width $\sigma_N/\langle S\rangle = \mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Limiting behaviour of the null distribution
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:T7-mass}.}
  \label{tab:T7-limits}
\end{table}

\begin{insight}[The $p\to\tfrac12$ recovery is a textbook check]
At $p=\tfrac12$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:T7-mass} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[\,S=s\,]\bigl|_{p=1/2}
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{s^2}{2N}\right],
\end{equation}
which is exactly the de~Moivre--Laplace approximation to the
$\pm 1$ random-walk endpoint distribution after $N$ steps. The
random-walk limit was a known result \emph{before} we did any of
this; recovering it from a parameter-dependent derivation is the
classical sanity check.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Sharpening the $(5,10)$ verdict}
\label{sec:T7-510}
We can now upgrade Task~5's informal ``$\sim 5\sigma_N$'' statement
into a calibrated $p$-value. For \texttt{sample\_small} we use the
pooled difficulty estimate $\hat p \approx 0.558$ and $N=25$, so
\begin{align}
  \langle S\rangle  &\;\approx\; 25\,(2(0.558)-1)^2 \;\approx\; 0.336,\\
  \sigma_N          &\;\approx\; \sqrt{25\bigl[1-(0.116)^4\bigr]}
                      \;\approx\; 5.000.
\end{align}
The observed pair has $s_{\text{obs}} = 25 = N$. Plugging into the
continuity-corrected tail formula Eq.~\eqref{eq:T7-tail}:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[\,S \geq 25\,]
  \;\approx\;
  1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{25 - 1 - 0.336}{5.000}\right)
  \;=\; 1 - \Phi(4.733)
  \;\approx\; 1.10\times 10^{-6}.
  \label{eq:pval-510}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Correcting for multiple comparisons.} We are not testing a
\emph{prespecified} pair; we are scanning all $\binom{M}{2} = 1225$
off-diagonal entries of $\Sm$ and picking the largest. A na\"ive
Bonferroni correction inflates the $p$-value by a factor of $1225$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{Bonferroni}}
  \;\approx\; 1225 \cdot 1.10\times 10^{-6}
  \;\approx\; 1.35\times 10^{-3}.
  \label{eq:bonf}
\end{equation}

\begin{keyresult}[Conviction-grade evidence against (5, 10)]
Even after the most conservative correction for scanning $1225$
candidate pairs, the probability that two \emph{honest} students would
achieve $S \geq 25$ on a $25$-question test of overall difficulty
$\hat p \approx 0.558$ is
$p_{\text{Bonferroni}} \approx 1.35\times 10^{-3}$ -- roughly one in
$740$. Compare to a conventional $\alpha = 0.01$ threshold: we reject
the null hypothesis of independence at better than the $1\%$ level
even \emph{after} accounting for multiple testing.
\end{keyresult}

The qualitative ``shortlist'' verdict of Task~4 has hardened into a
quantitative rejection of the null. The pair $(5,10)$ is now flagged
not just as a geometric outlier in $\Sm$ but as a statistical
impossibility under independent test-taking.

\subsection{Looking ahead to Task 8}
The homogeneous-$p$ derivation above suffices for
\texttt{sample\_small}, where the empirical column means are not too
dispersed. For \texttt{sample\_larger} ($M=10{,}000$, $N=50$) we have
no reason to expect questions to share difficulty, and the
identically-distributed assumption in (ii) of the CLT box fails.

The fix is to drop (ii) and invoke the more general
\emph{Lindeberg--Feller CLT}, which only requires (i)~independence,
(iii)~finite variance \emph{plus} a uniform smallness condition on
individual variances. Both extensions hold trivially: the
$Y_k = X_{ik}X_{jk}$ remain bounded by $1$, so the Lindeberg
condition is automatic.

The mean and variance generalise term-by-term:
\begin{equation}
  \langle S\rangle \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N}(2p_k-1)^2,
  \qquad
  \sigma_N^2 \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl[1-(2p_k-1)^4\bigr],
  \label{eq:T8-moments}
\end{equation}
where $p_k$ is the empirical column-mean accuracy on question $k$.
Equations~\eqref{eq:T7-mass}--\eqref{eq:T7-tail} retain their
Gaussian form; only the moments are replaced by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:T8-moments}. Task~8 then computes
$z_{ij} = (S_{ij} - \langle S\rangle)/\sigma_N$ for every pair and
flags those exceeding a threshold corrected for the
$\binom{10{,}000}{2} \approx 5\times 10^7$ candidate comparisons.

\subsection{The dataset and what changes}
The second slice of the archive, \texttt{sample\_larger}, is the
design matrix
\begin{equation}
  \X^{(\text{large})} \;\in\; \{-1,+1\}^{M\times N},
  \qquad M = 10{,}000, \qquad N = 50.
\end{equation}
Three structural facts about it force a redesign of the test pipeline
from Tasks 6--7:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous question difficulty.} The empirical
        column means $\hat p_k = \tfrac{1}{M}\sum_i \mathbf{1}\{X_{ik}=+1\}$
        span the range $[0.4903, 0.9015]$, with $46$ of the $50$
        questions essentially at coin-flip difficulty
        ($\hat p_k \in [0.49, 0.55]$) and $4$ ``easy'' questions
        clustered around $\hat p_k \approx 0.90$. The homogeneous-$p$
        assumption of Task~7's CLT is therefore violated.
  \item \textbf{Multiple-comparisons inflation.} The number of
        candidate off-diagonal pairs grows from $\binom{50}{2}=1{,}225$
        on \texttt{sample\_small} to
        $\binom{10{,}000}{2}=49{,}995{,}000$ on \texttt{sample\_larger}
        -- a factor of $\sim\!40{,}000$. Any per-pair threshold that
        was conservative on the small dataset is wildly anti-conservative
        on the larger one, so we will need a tail probability whose
        per-pair value lies below $1/(M(M-1)/2)$ for a Bonferroni-style
        family-wise control.
  \item \textbf{Group structure, not just pair structure.} On a class
        of $50$ students, the natural unit of investigation is a pair;
        on a cohort of $10{,}000$ it is a \emph{clique} of students who
        share a single answer vector. We will detect both.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Per-question difficulty estimate}
The empirical column means $\hat p_k$ act as the maximum-likelihood
estimators of the per-question success probabilities under the null
$H_0$ that students are independent. With $M=10{,}000$ the sampling
error on each $\hat p_k$ is
$\sqrt{\hat p_k(1-\hat p_k)/M} \lesssim 0.005$, so they are essentially
the true $p_k$ for our purposes.

\begin{insight}[Two distinct difficulty regimes]
The cohort sees an exam with two distinct populations of questions: a
``bulk'' of $46$ near-chance questions ($p_k\!\approx\!0.5$) and a small
set of $4$ near-trivial questions ($p_k\!\approx\!0.9$). The bulk
contributes essentially $\sigma_Y^2 = 1$ per question to $\Var(S)$ and
$0$ to $\langle S\rangle$. The four easy questions each contribute
$(2(0.9)-1)^2 = 0.64$ to $\langle S\rangle$ and only $0.59$ to
$\Var(S)$. The net null mean is therefore around
$4 \cdot 0.64 = 2.56$, and the variance is around $46 + 4\cdot 0.59
= 48.4$; we will see both numbers emerge exactly below.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Closed-form null moments (Lindeberg--Feller)}
Dropping the identically-distributed assumption (ii) of the
Lindeberg--L\'evy CLT used in Task~7 and keeping (i)~independence and
(iii)~finite, bounded variance, the Lindeberg--Feller CLT gives
exactly the same Gaussian conclusion -- but with the moments now
summed over the heterogeneous $p_k$. Equation~\eqref{eq:T8-moments}
specialises to the empirical values
\begin{align}
  \langle S\rangle_{H_0}
    &\;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{50}\,(2\hat p_k - 1)^2 \;=\; 2.5567,
    \label{eq:T8-mu-numerical}\\
  \sigma_{H_0}^2
    &\;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{50}\,\bigl[\,1 - (2\hat p_k - 1)^4\,\bigr] \;=\; 48.3704,
    \nonumber\\
  \sigma_{H_0}
    &\;=\; 6.9549.
    \label{eq:T8-sigma-numerical}
\end{align}
Two reassuring cross-checks: (a) an empirical sample of $5{,}000$
random pairs from \texttt{sample\_larger} yields
$\bar S_{\text{emp}}\!=\!2.60$ and
$\hat\sigma_{S,\text{emp}}\!=\!6.83$, both within sampling error of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:T8-mu-numerical}--\eqref{eq:T8-sigma-numerical};
(b) the Task~7 ``homogeneous-$p$'' shortcut with the pooled
$\bar p = 0.532$ would have given $\langle S\rangle = 50\cdot(0.064)^2
= 0.20$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{50\cdot(1 - 0.064^4)} = 7.07$, missing the
true mean by an order of magnitude because it averages over the very
non-uniform $p_k$ landscape.

\subsection{Exact null distribution: a shifted Poisson binomial}
\label{sec:t8-pmf}
Task~6 showed that under homogeneous $p$ the null distribution of $S$
is \emph{exactly} a shifted binomial, $S = 2T - N$ with
$T\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N, q)$, $q=p^2+(1-p)^2$. In the heterogeneous
setting the same change-of-variables works, but the binomial is
replaced by a Poisson binomial:
\begin{equation}
  S \;=\; 2K \;-\; N,
  \qquad
  K \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{Y_k=+1\},
  \qquad
  K \;\sim\; \mathrm{PoiBin}(q_1,\ldots,q_N),
  \label{eq:T8-poisson-binomial}
\end{equation}
where the per-question match probabilities are
\begin{equation}
  q_k \;\equiv\; \Pr[Y_k = +1] \;=\; p_k^2 + (1-p_k)^2 \;=\; 1 - 2p_k(1-p_k).
  \label{eq:T8-qk}
\end{equation}
A Poisson binomial is the distribution of a sum of independent but
non-identically distributed Bernoullis. Its PMF is computed in closed
form by sequential convolution: starting from
$P^{(0)} = (1)$ (probability $1$ at zero matches), each new question
updates
\begin{equation}
  P^{(k)}[i] \;=\; (1 - q_k)\,P^{(k-1)}[i] \;+\; q_k\,P^{(k-1)}[i-1],
  \qquad i = 0,1,\ldots,k,
  \label{eq:T8-convolve}
\end{equation}
which is the standard polynomial-multiplication recurrence in
$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ time and $\mathcal{O}(N)$ memory.\footnote{For
$N=50$ this is microseconds; for very large $N$ a Fourier-domain
$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ variant is available, but the
naive recurrence is preferred at this scale because it is
floating-point stable to better than $10^{-15}$.} The full PMF
$\Pr[\,K=k\,]$ for $k=0,\ldots,N$ is then in hand, and tail
probabilities follow from
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\bigl[\,S \geq s\,\bigr]
  \;=\; \Pr\Bigl[\,K \geq \bigl\lceil (s+N)/2 \bigr\rceil\,\Bigr]
  \;=\; \sum_{k = \lceil (s+N)/2\rceil}^{N}\Pr[\,K=k\,].
  \label{eq:T8-tail}
\end{equation}
The ceiling absorbs any parity mismatch between $s$ and $N$ (recall
that $S$ takes values of the same parity as $N$, here even).

\begin{insight}[Why the exact PMF beats the CLT here]
The Task~7 Gaussian approximation has a continuity-corrected accuracy
of $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ in the bulk. In the deep tail, the error
is dominated by skewness and grows multiplicatively: at $5\sigma$ the
Gaussian under-counts a positively skewed tail by roughly
$\exp(\kappa_3 z^3 / 6)$ where $\kappa_3$ is the standardised
third cumulant. For our $p_k$ landscape, $\kappa_3 \approx 0.13$, so
the Gaussian at $z = 6.8$ \emph{under-states} the true tail by a
factor of $\sim e^{2}$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:T8-poisson-binomial} sidesteps
this entirely: it is the exact null distribution, computed in
closed-form.
\end{insight}

\begin{equation}
  \Exp\bigl[\,\#\{i<j: S_{ij}\geq s\}\bigm|H_0\bigr]

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Pair scan at $\boldsymbol{S\geq 40}$}{Pair scan at S >= 40}}
We compute the full set of Gram-matrix entries
$S_{ij} = \langle\X_i,\X_j\rangle$ for $i<j$ by block matrix
multiplication\footnote{The pair scan is implemented in
\texttt{scan\_high\_similarity\_pairs} (\texttt{src/task8.py}). The
$10{,}000\times 10{,}000$ Gram matrix is $\sim 400$\,MB at int32; we
avoid materialising it by streaming 500-row blocks at int16 (max value
$\pm 50$ fits comfortably) and filtering each block on the fly.}
and report every pair above the threshold $s=40$.

\begin{keyresult}[Pair scan result]
The scan returns \textbf{exactly $10$ pairs}, all of which lie at the
theoretical maximum $S_{ij}=N=50$. Their per-pair exact null tail
probability is $\Pr[\,S\geq 50\mid H_0\,]=6.4\times 10^{-15}$ -- a
$6.82\,\sigma_{H_0}$ event -- and the expected number of such pairs
under $H_0$ over all $\binom{10{,}000}{2}\approx 5\times 10^{7}$
candidates is $3.2\times 10^{-7}$. After the most conservative
Bonferroni inflation by the full pair count, the family-wise $p$-value
of the joint observation remains
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{FWER}}
  \;\leq\;\binom{M}{2}\cdot\Pr[\,S\geq 50\mid H_0\,]
  \;\approx\; 3.2\times 10^{-7},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ better than $3{,}000{,}000$:$1$ against the null even after
accounting for the entire $\sim\!5\times 10^7$-fold scan.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Promoting pairs to a clique: the duplicate-row scan}
Reading off the $10$ flagged pairs by their student indices reveals a
strong structural pattern:
\begin{equation}
  \{(i,j):S_{ij}=50\}
  \;=\;\bigl\{(a,b):a,b\in\mathcal{C},\,a<b\bigr\},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{C} \;=\; \{86, 1352, 1907, 3543, 5363\},
\end{equation}
in one-based student IDs ($\{85, 1351, 1906, 3542, 5362\}$
zero-based). The $\binom{5}{2}=10$ pairs are precisely the pairwise
comparisons among a five-student set, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:T8-tail}
(at $s=N$) can only achieve $S_{ij}=N$ when their answer vectors are
byte-identical. A direct sweep with \texttt{np.unique} on the rows of
$\X^{(\text{large})}$ confirms this: the five students share a single
common answer vector (and no other duplicate group exists in the
dataset). This is a structural fact that the pairwise scan alone
cannot state cleanly.

\begin{insight}[Why the group-level statement is stronger]
A pair $(i,j)$ with $S_{ij}=N$ proves that two students gave identical
answers. A clique $\mathcal{C}$ of size $k$ with all
$\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise similarities at $N$ proves that all $k$
students gave the same identical answer vector -- a single shared
source. The exact null probability that a \emph{specific} set of $k$
students share an answer vector under independent test-taking is
\[
  \Pr\bigl[\,X_{i_1}\!=\!\cdots\!=\!X_{i_k}\bigm|H_0\bigr]
  \;=\;\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}\bigl[\,p_\ell^{\,k}+(1-p_\ell)^{k}\,\bigr],
\]
which for our $\hat p_k$ and $k=5$ evaluates to roughly
$1.8\times 10^{-27}$. Multiplied by the
$\binom{10{,}000}{5}\approx 8\times 10^{17}$ ways to choose any five
students, the expected number of such cliques under $H_0$ is on the
order of $10^{-9}$ -- absolutely incompatible with the existence of
the observed clique.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Verdict}
Both routes through Task~8 -- the pair scan and the duplicate-row
scan -- agree, and both reject the null at levels that no realistic
multiple-testing correction can rescue.

\begin{keyresult}[Task 8 final answer]
The \texttt{sample\_larger} cohort contains \textbf{one colluding
group} consisting of the five students with one-based IDs
\[
  \mathcal{C} \;=\; \{86,\;1352,\;1907,\;3543,\;5363\}.
\]
Their answer vectors are pairwise identical
($S_{ij}=N=50$ for all $\binom{5}{2}=10$ pairs), with exact one-sided
null tail probability $6.4\times 10^{-15}$ per pair and a
family-wise $p$-value $\leq 3.2\times 10^{-7}$ after the conservative
Bonferroni correction for $\binom{10{,}000}{2}\approx 5\times 10^7$
candidate comparisons. No other pair in the dataset exceeds the
permissive threshold $S\geq 40$. The pipeline that began in Task~1
with a heatmap of $\X$ closes here with a fully calibrated
verdict on the larger dataset.
\end{keyresult}
